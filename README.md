# Elmas Lab

## Engineering reliable AI for biomedical discovery

Welcome to the **Elmas Lab**. We work at the intersection of electrical and electronics engineering, machine learning, signal processing, optimization, and biomedical data science.

Our goal is to develop computational methods that extract reliable biological and clinical information from complex data—including genomic sequences, molecular measurements, digital pathology images, and spatial omics data.

> **Our central theme:** Solving inverse problems under uncertainty in biomedical data: inferring hidden biological states from noisy, incomplete, and indirect measurements; quantifying the reliability of those inferences; and developing new methodological frameworks for biomedical research.

The lab is primarily computational. We work with public biomedical datasets, ethically approved clinical data, and open-source software rather than wet-lab experiments.

## Research directions

### 1. Population-aware genomic AI

Genetic tests often identify variants whose clinical significance is uncertain. We develop statistical and machine-learning approaches to improve genetic-variant interpretation, with particular interest in population-aware models for the Turkish genome.

**Example topics**

- Bayesian fusion of genomic and clinical evidence
- Calibration of pathogenicity-prediction models
- Conformal prediction, uncertainty quantification, and abstaining classifiers
- Genomic data engineering and open-source clinical software
- Machine learning for rare-disease and hereditary-cancer genetics

**Related work**

- [Discovering genome-wide tagSNPs](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0167994)
- [Sparse dictionary selection for haplotype inference](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2164-14-645)
- [Circulating biomarkers and disease risks](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms25137376)

Our objective is not only to classify a variant, but also to identify when the available evidence is inadequate for a reliable decision.

### 2. AI for digital pathology and proteomics

We study whether routine H&E pathology slides can provide useful clues about molecular states that normally require costly proteomic assays. Using pathology foundation models and machine learning, we investigate the prediction of protein activity, pathway states, and potentially druggable cancer targets from digital tissue images.

**Example topics**

- Whole-slide image processing and computer vision
- Pathology foundation models and transfer learning
- Multiple-instance learning and attention-based models
- Image–omics integration using CPTAC and other public datasets
- Visualization of tissue regions driving molecular predictions

**Related work**

- [Clinical benchmark of public self-supervised pathology foundation models](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-58796-1)
- [Pan-cancer proteogenomic analysis of post-transcriptional kinase targets](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-021-02636-7)
- [Proteomic therapeutic-target analysis in hepatocellular carcinoma](https://doi.org/10.3389/fonc.2022.814120)

The aim is to create cost-aware tools that prioritize samples for deeper molecular testing—not to suggest that image-based models replace proteomic assays.

### 3. Spatial omics and inverse problems

Spatial transcriptomics measures gene expression at locations across a tissue, but each measurement may contain a mixture of several cell types. We frame recovery of the underlying cellular composition as a signal-processing and constrained-optimization problem.

**Example topics**

- Sparse recovery and dictionary selection
- Convex optimization, ADMM, and proximal algorithms
- Spatial regularization and edge-preserving reconstruction
- Count-data models for genomic measurements
- Uncertainty quantification for ill-posed inverse problems
- Open-source methods for spatial-transcriptomics analysis

**Related work**

- [Sparse dictionary selection for haplotype inference](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2164-14-240)
- [Gene-regulatory-network reconstruction from incomplete measurements](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13637-017-0068-1)

This direction is particularly suitable for students interested in mathematical modeling, optimization, statistical signal processing, and algorithm development.

### 4. AI for precision oncology

We develop computational methods that combine RNA, protein, genomic, and clinical data to discover cancer biomarkers and predict treatment response, including response to cancer immunotherapy.

**Example topics**

- Multi-omics integration and biomarker discovery
- Protein–RNA modeling and post-transcriptional regulation
- Machine learning for immunotherapy-response prediction
- Survival analysis, model calibration, and external validation
- Reproducible clinical-AI pipelines and health-software development

**Related work**

- [OPPTI: Pan-cancer identification of post-transcriptional kinase targets](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-021-02636-7)
- [Multiscale protein networks for identifying oncogenic regulators](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13045-023-01531-9)
- [Mutation impact on mRNA versus protein expression](https://doi.org/10.1093/gigascience/giae100)
- [Expression-driven genetic dependencies for precision oncology](https://doi.org/10.1093/gigascience/giae110)

## Methods and skills

Students may gain practical experience with:

- Python, R, MATLAB, Linux, Git, containers, and reproducible research workflows
- Machine learning, deep learning, and foundation models
- Bayesian inference, probability, statistics, and uncertainty quantification
- Signal processing, optimization, sparse modeling, and inverse problems
- Computer vision and biomedical image processing
- Genomic, proteomic, and spatial-omics data analysis
- Software engineering, benchmarking, and open-source development

The lab’s work is grounded in rigorous model development and evaluation. We value interpretability, calibration, external validation, transparent code, reproducibility, and honest reporting of limitations—not predictive accuracy alone.

## For prospective MS students

The Elmas Lab welcomes MS students in:

- Electrical and Electronics Engineering
- Computer Engineering
- Biomedical Engineering
- Artificial Intelligence and Data Science
- Applied Mathematics and related quantitative fields

Previous training in biology is helpful but not required. Students interested in programming, probability and statistics, signals and systems, machine learning, optimization, image processing, or data-driven biomedical research are particularly encouraged to explore the lab’s work.

A typical MS thesis aims to make a concrete technical contribution, such as:

- A new algorithm or mathematical model
- A reproducible software package
- A rigorous benchmark study
- A curated dataset or analysis pipeline
- A validated, publishable computational model

## Research principles

- **Rigor:** Design methods and evaluations that address a clear scientific question.
- **Reliability:** Quantify uncertainty and avoid overconfident predictions.
- **Reproducibility:** Share documented code, data-processing workflows, and evaluation protocols whenever possible.
- **Translation:** Develop tools that address real constraints in biomedical research and clinical practice.
- **Collaboration:** Connect engineering methods with expertise in medicine, pathology, oncology, genetics, and bioinformatics.
- **Curiosity:** 🐾🔬 Curiosity does not harm science-minded cats. Ask new questions, test ideas carefully, and let each answer lead to the next question.
- **Optimism:** 🌱🔬 Persist through the negative results; progress is inevitable.
